Henry Adams is a real person. But his life story is created from the statistical anomaly of the Eastern Woodlands in 1838 CE.

Henry Brooks Adams was born in 1838 CE, in the Eastern Woodlands, near Boston, under the shadow of the State House. Boston was a city of about 93,000 people. He lived in a large town house with servants, and in summer at Quincy, seven miles off. Of seven children, he was the fourth. His father died when he was 48; his mother when he was 51. His table was mostly wheat bread and maize, pork and dairy, and potatoes and garden vegetables, with seasonal apples, and the meat and fine fare that only a household of rank could command; the one meal he remembered was a baked apple, brought to him at 3 as he came back from scarlet fever. He could read and write. The fever left him two or three inches shorter than his brothers; he stood about 160 cm (5 ft 3 in). His brothers, he wrote, were the type; he was the variation.

He married at 34. At 47, he was widowed. He had no children. His journeys took him as far as about 10,000 miles, to Sydney, by way of Samoa, Tahiti, and Fiji. He spoke English, German, and French, and read medieval Latin. He was christened a Unitarian and ended an unbeliever, devoted late in life to the Virgin of Chartres. He worked as what the record can only call a merchant, official, or professional man: a private secretary, a journalist, a professor of history at Harvard, a historian. He lived on rents and dues drawn from other people's work; his mother's father died the richest man in Boston. He reckoned that probably no child born in the year held better cards than he, and could not decide whether the cards were marked. He was not consulted, but would have told them to change nothing.

At 6, the Boston and Albany Railroad opened, the first Cunard steamers stood in the bay, and a telegraph carried the news of the 1844 nominations; he dated the end of his eighteenth century from that spring. His grandfather, who had been President, died when he was 10. The American Civil War began when he was 23; he spent it in London. His sister Louisa died when he was 32, of tetanus after a carriage accident in Italy. When he was 45, Krakatoa in the Sunda Strait erupted far away, and for months afterwards the sunsets ran red. Halley's Comet crossed the sky when he was 72.

Death came at 80, in his sleep, at his house on Lafayette Square in Washington; no cause was recorded. His body was buried, as the churches then required, beside his wife in Rock Creek Cemetery, under a bronze figure he had commissioned and refused to name; the grave carries no inscription. His line ended with him. The account of his life that he had printed for a hundred friends was published that autumn.
